Solo vs. Group Adventures: Which One Is Right for You?

We built this company around the idea that traveling solo shouldn't mean traveling alone. That distinction is basically the whole answer to this question, but it's worth breaking down properly, because solo and group trips genuinely give you different things, and neither one is the better option across the board.

What Solo Travel Actually Gives You

Nobody to negotiate the schedule with. You linger at the waterfall as long as you want, skip the market stop if you're tired, decide on a whim to add an extra day in Kampot. Solo travel also has a way of pushing you into conversations you wouldn't otherwise have, with guides, with other travelers, with people in the village you're passing through. There's a specific kind of confidence that comes from handling a new place on your own terms.

The tradeoff is that certain trips are genuinely harder or riskier to do completely alone, remote treks, for example, where having other people around matters for both safety and morale on long, difficult days.

Cascading falls in Kampot — photo by field guide Lisa

What Group Travel Actually Gives You

A group changes the emotional shape of a hard trip. Aural Mountain on your own is a personal test. Aural Mountain with a group is a shared one, someone hands you water when you're flagging, someone else is the reason you laugh instead of quit on the steep section. Group trips also tend to open doors solo travel doesn't, easier conversations with strangers who become friends by day two, shared costs on things like transport, and less of the mental load of figuring out logistics alone.

The tradeoff is less flexibility. You move at the group's pace, not yours.

Why "Solo" Doesn't Have to Mean Alone

This is the actual point of what we do. Every one of our trips, whether you show up as a solo traveler or as part of a group booking, puts you with a local guide and, in most cases, other travelers doing the same trip. You get the independence of traveling solo, no one waiting on you back home, no shared itinerary to negotiate before you even leave, combined with the built-in company of a group once you're on the ground.

How to Decide

If you're craving total control over your pace and want space to think, lean solo, but pick a trip with a guide if the terrain calls for it. If you want the shared-hardship kind of memory, the ones that turn into inside jokes for years, lean group. Either way, you don't have to choose between adventure and company. That's exactly the gap we exist to close.
